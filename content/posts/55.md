Summary: A look at the growing divide in the C++ community between modern, tool‑driven practices and legacy, compatibility‑focused usage.

- **A Divided C++ Landscape:** The notion of a unified, dialect‑free C++ is largely considered dead; instead, two dominant camps have emerged 
- **Modern Camp:** Tech‑savvy organizations (e.g. large greenfield teams) adopt C++17+, strong tooling, automated builds from versioned source, linters, formatters, static analyzers, and treat codebases as long‑term assets
- **Legacy Camp:** Older or more conservative environments rely on archaic code, opaque binary dependencies, minimal tooling, ABI‑stability obsession, and limited ability to build from source
- **Tooling as the Fault Line:** The gap between factions hinges less on language features and more on infrastructure—modern tooling enables migration and safety, while legacy environments struggle to evolve
- **Committee Constraints:** C++ standards maintain backward compatibility (no ABI breaks, no “viral annotations”) and prioritize zero‑cost abstractions—choices that favor legacy usage and hinder radical improvements
- **Profiles vs Safe C++:** Safety Profiles aim to improve legacy C++ without requiring breaking changes, while proposals like Sean Baxter’s Safe C++ would essentially introduce a new, Rust‑like language—making them incompatible with the established committee approach
- **Community Debate:** As one Redditor summarized:  
  > "When it comes to the current schism on how to improve the safety of C++ there are largely two factions: *The Bjarne/Herb side... minimal changes... unclear memory safety* vs *Safe C++, a whole‑sale adoption of Rust's semantics... effectively a new language*.
